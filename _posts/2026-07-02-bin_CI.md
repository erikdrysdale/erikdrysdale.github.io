Comparing two independent proportions is one of the most common inferential tasks in statistics. Does treatment A outperform treatment B? Is the event rate different across subgroups? The textbook approach — a chi-squared test or a two-proportion z-test — relies on a Gaussian approximation that can fail badly at small sample sizes or extreme proportions. Modern Python addresses this: `scipy.stats` ships `barnard_exact`, `boschloo_exact`, and `fisher_exact`, and `statsmodels` provides the Newcomb score confidence interval for a proportion difference via `confint_proportions_2indep`. Together these cover most applied use cases.

But a question these tools do not directly address is: *given your data, what exactly is your type-I error rate?* In the single-binomial case, the discreteness of the distribution makes every exact test *knowably* conservative — the CDF-based prediction of the type-I error is always exact. This post examines whether the same property carries over to the two-sample case. The short answer is: only when the null proportion \\(\pi_0\\) is known. When \\(\pi_0\\) must be estimated from the data using the pooled proportion \\(\hat\pi\\), the conservatism becomes *unknowable* — the prediction from the estimated null distribution is a biased and unreliable proxy for the true rejection rate.

## (1) Single-binomial exact inference

Suppose we observe \\(y \sim \text{Bin}(n, \pi)\\) and wish to test \\(H_0: \pi = \pi_0\\) against the two-sided alternative \\(H_1: \pi \neq \pi_0\\) at level \\(\alpha\\). The natural rejection region is

$$
\text{Reject} \iff y < k_\ell \quad \text{or} \quad y > k_u
$$

for integer-valued bounds \\(k_\ell \leq k_u\\). We want the probability of rejection under \\(H_0\\) to be at most \\(\alpha\\). Because the binomial is discrete, we cannot generally achieve equality, so we choose bounds that are as tight as possible while respecting the constraint. The standard approach inverts the CDF conservatively:

$$
k_u = F^{-1}(1 - \alpha/2;\, n, \pi_0), \qquad k_\ell = F^{-1}(\alpha/2;\, n, \pi_0) + 1
$$

where \\(F^{-1}(\cdot;\, n, \pi_0)\\) denotes the *conservative* quantile: the smallest integer \\(k^*\\) such that \\(F(k^*) \geq q\\) for \\(q \geq 0.5\\), and the largest \\(k^*\\) such that \\(F(k^*) \leq q\\) for \\(q < 0.5\\). I implement this as `qbinom`:

```python
def qbinom(alpha: float, n: int, pi: float) -> int:
    kstar = int(binom.ppf(q=alpha, n=n, p=pi))
    Fk = binom.cdf(k=kstar, n=n, p=pi)
    check = bool(np.where(alpha > 0.5, Fk >= alpha, Fk <= alpha))
    while not check:
        kstar = int(np.where(alpha > 0.5, kstar + 1, kstar - 1))
        Fk = binom.cdf(k=kstar, n=n, p=pi)
        check = bool(np.where(alpha > 0.5, Fk >= alpha, Fk <= alpha))
    return kstar
```

Note: `scipy.stats.binom.ppf` does not always return the conservative quantile due to floating-point rounding, so the while loop corrects for this.[[^1]]

The type-I error for this test is \\(F(k_\ell - 1;\, n, \pi_0) + 1 - F(k_u;\, n, \pi_0)\\), which is strictly ≤ \\(\alpha\\) and — crucially — is exactly computable from the binomial CDF. There is no simulation required to determine by how much the test is conservative.

Figure 1A shows coverage (= 1 − type-I error) for a 90% level test across \\(\pi \in (0.05, 0.95)\\) and several sample sizes. Coverage always meets or exceeds 90%, but can be substantially higher at small \\(n\\).

Figure 1B confirms the *knowability* of this conservatism directly: the analytically predicted type-I error tracks the simulated actual type-I error with near-perfect accuracy across all \\(\pi\\) and \\(n\\) values. The points cluster tightly on the 45-degree line.

### (1b) Connection to Clopper-Pearson

The Clopper-Pearson confidence interval takes the complementary view: rather than fixing \\(\pi_0\\) and computing rejection bounds in \\(k\\)-space, it asks which values of \\(\pi_0\\) would *not* be rejected given the observed \\(k\\). This yields the interval

$$
\left[ F_{\text{Beta}}^{-1}\!\left(\frac{\alpha}{2};\, k,\, n-k+1\right),\; F_{\text{Beta}}^{-1}\!\left(1-\frac{\alpha}{2};\, k+1,\, n-k\right) \right]
$$

The `qbinom` pivot CI takes a different route: fix \\(\pi_0\\), compute the symmetric acceptance region \\([k_\ell, k_u]\\), and shift the bounds to be centred on \\(\hat\pi = k/n\\):

$$
\left[ \hat\pi - \left(\frac{k_u}{n} - \pi_0\right),\quad \hat\pi + \left(\pi_0 - \frac{k_\ell}{n}\right) \right]
$$

Despite their different derivations, both intervals are numerically identical — they match the CI returned by `scipy.stats.binomtest(k, n, method='exact')` exactly. Figure 1C overlays the lower and upper bounds from both methods for \\(n=20\\), \\(\pi_0=0.5\\), confirming perfect agreement.

<center><p><img src="/figures/bin_CI_fig1c_cp_equivalence.png" width="65%"></p></center>

The reason this connection matters is that there is *no two-sample analogue of Clopper-Pearson*. A CI for a proportion difference cannot be obtained by direct CDF inversion over a single parameter, because the joint distribution of \\((y_1, y_2)\\) depends on both \\(\pi_1\\) and \\(\pi_2\\) separately. The `qbinom` framing, however, does generalise: we simply replace the single-binomial quantile function with one derived from the distribution of \\(\delta = y_1 - y_2\\), as developed in Sections 4 and 5.

## (2) Joint distribution of two independent binomials

Let \\(y_1 \sim \text{Bin}(n_1, \pi_1)\\) and \\(y_2 \sim \text{Bin}(n_2, \pi_2)\\) be independent. The joint PMF factorises as

$$
P(y_1 = k_1,\; y_2 = k_2) = \binom{n_1}{k_1}\pi_1^{k_1}(1-\pi_1)^{n_1-k_1} \cdot \binom{n_2}{k_2}\pi_2^{k_2}(1-\pi_2)^{n_2-k_2}
$$

I implement this as `dist_binom2`, which pre-computes the full PMF and CDF over the discrete support \\(\{0, \ldots, n_1\} \times \{0, \ldots, n_2\}\\) by taking outer products of the two marginal PMFs and CDFs:

```python
class dist_binom2:
    def __init__(self, n1: int, pi1: float, n2: int, pi2: float):
        k1, k2 = np.arange(n1 + 1), np.arange(n2 + 1)
        pmf_mat = np.outer(binom.pmf(k2, n2, pi2), binom.pmf(k1, n1, pi1))
        cdf_mat = np.outer(binom.cdf(k2, n2, pi2), binom.cdf(k1, n1, pi1))
        # stored as tidy DataFrame with columns v1, v2, pmf, cdf
```

Figure 2 shows the joint PMF for \\(n_1=10\\), \\(n_2=15\\) across a 3×3 grid of \\((\pi_1, \pi_2)\\) values. When \\(\pi\\) is close to 0 or 1 the mass is concentrated near a corner of the grid; around \\(\pi = 0.5\\) it spreads across a broad diagonal band.

<center><p><img src="/figures/bin_CI_fig2_joint_pmf.png" width="80%"></p></center>

An important observation: the marginal difference \\(\delta = y_1 - y_2\\) does not uniquely determine the joint distribution. A given \\(\delta\\) can arise from many \\((k_1, k_2)\\) pairs, and the probability of each pair depends on both \\(\pi_1\\) and \\(\pi_2\\) separately. Under \\(H_0: \pi_1 = \pi_2 = \pi_0\\) the distribution collapses to a single-parameter family — but that parameter \\(\pi_0\\) still needs to be specified.

## (3) What Python offers today

Python has excellent modern tools for testing \\(H_0: \pi_1 = \pi_2\\). The `scipy.stats` module provides three exact tests and `statsmodels` provides a score-based CI. In R, the `exact2x2` package is the standard reference for unconditional exact tests. A brief taxonomy of the Python tools:

- **Fisher's exact test** (`fisher_exact`): conditions on both row and column margins, using the hypergeometric null distribution. Computationally simple and widely used, but conditioning discards information.
- **Barnard's exact test** (`barnard_exact`): does not condition on column margins; instead it maximises the p-value over the nuisance proportion. Uniformly more powerful than Fisher for two-sided unconditional tests.[[^2]]
- **Boschloo's exact test** (`boschloo_exact`): combines Barnard's unconditional framework with Fisher's statistic, achieving high power while retaining Fisher's interpretable p-value.
- **Newcomb score CI** (`confint_proportions_2indep`, `method='newcomb'`): applies the Wilson score method to each proportion separately and combines. Approximate rather than exact; often works well at moderate \\(n\\) but can slightly inflate type-I error at small \\(n\\) or extreme \\(\pi\\).

Figure 3 shows empirical type-I error under \\(H_0\\) across three \\((n_1, n_2)\\) settings. All three exact tests stay at or below \\(\alpha = 10\%\\) for all \\(\pi\\). The Newcomb score CI, being approximate, slightly exceeds \\(\alpha\\) at small \\(n\\) and extreme proportions — a known limitation of the method.

<center><p><img src="/figures/bin_CI_fig3_method_comparison.png" width="95%"></p></center>

None of these tools directly answers the question: *given my data, what is my exact conservative type-I error?* That is what the next two sections develop.

## (4) Exact testing when the null proportion is known

Under \\(H_0: \pi_1 = \pi_2 = \pi_0\\) with \\(\pi_0\\) known, the marginal distribution of \\(\delta = y_1 - y_2\\) is fully determined. Its support is \\(\{-n_2, \ldots, n_1\}\\) and its PMF is the discrete convolution of \\(\text{Bin}(n_1, \pi_0)\\) and \\(-\text{Bin}(n_2, \pi_0)\\):

$$
P(\delta = d) = \sum_{k=0}^{n_1} P(y_1 = k)\, P(y_2 = k - d)
$$

where \\(P(y_2 = k-d) = 0\\) whenever \\(k-d \notin \{0, \ldots, n_2\}\\). I implement this as `binom_diff`:

```python
class binom_diff:
    def __init__(self, n1: int, pi0: float, n2: int):
        k1, k2 = np.arange(n1 + 1), np.arange(n2 + 1)
        pmf1 = binom.pmf(k=k1, n=n1, p=pi0)
        pmf2 = binom.pmf(k=k2, n=n2, p=pi0)
        delta_min, delta_max = -n2, n1
        pmf_delta = np.zeros(delta_max - delta_min + 1)
        for i1, v1 in enumerate(k1):
            for i2, v2 in enumerate(k2):
                pmf_delta[v1 - v2 - delta_min] += pmf1[i1] * pmf2[i2]
        self._cdf = np.minimum(np.cumsum(pmf_delta), 1.0)
```

Figure 4A shows the PMF of \\(\delta\\) for four \\((n_1, n_2, \pi_0)\\) combinations. The distribution is symmetric around zero when \\(n_1 = n_2\\), and increasingly concentrated when \\(\pi_0\\) is far from 0.5 (because both marginal binomials have reduced variance).

<center><p><img src="/figures/bin_CI_fig4a_binom_diff_pmf.png" width="80%"></p></center>

The two-sided test rejects when \\(\delta < k_\ell\\) or \\(\delta > k_u\\), where the bounds are obtained by applying the same conservative quantile inversion as in Section 1 — now applied to the `binom_diff` CDF. The predicted type-I error is \\(F(k_\ell - 1) + 1 - F(k_u)\\), exactly computable with no simulation required.

Figure 4B confirms the knowable-conservatism property: across all combinations of \\(\pi_0 \in (0.1, 0.9)\\) and nominal levels \\(\alpha \in (0.01, 0.25)\\) for \\(n_1=20\\), \\(n_2=25\\), the analytically predicted type-I error tracks the simulated actual error precisely. The points lie on the 45-degree line — the same structure as Figure 1B for the single binomial.

<center><p><img src="/figures/bin_CI_fig4b_exact_null_t1.png" width="60%"></p></center>

## (5) The unknowability problem

In practice \\(\pi_0\\) is unknown. The natural approach is to estimate it with the pooled proportion \\(\hat\pi = (y_1 + y_2)/(n_1 + n_2)\\), construct the rejection bounds \\(\hat{k}_\ell\\), \\(\hat{k}_u\\) from `binom_diff`\\((n_1, \hat\pi, n_2)\\), and predict the type-I error as \\(\hat{F}(\hat{k}_\ell - 1) + 1 - \hat{F}(\hat{k}_u)\\) where \\(\hat{F}\\) is the corresponding CDF.

The problem is that the rejection bounds now *depend on the data*, because \\(\hat\pi\\) is a function of \\((y_1, y_2)\\) — the same observations used to compute \\(\delta\\). This makes \\(\hat{k}_\ell\\) and \\(\hat{k}_u\\) random variables correlated with \\(\delta\\), introducing an additional source of randomness that the CDF prediction cannot account for. The stated conservatism is no longer verifiable from the data.

Figure 5A illustrates this for \\(n_1=20\\), \\(n_2=25\\), true \\(\pi=0.5\\), \\(\alpha=10\%\\). Samples are binned by their observed \\(\hat\pi\\). The red line shows the average CDF-based prediction within each \\(\hat\pi\\) bin; the blue line shows the actual rejection rate. Near the centre (\\(\hat\pi \approx 0.5\\)) the two lines agree, but at the extremes — where \\(\hat\pi\\) is far from the true \\(\pi\\) — the prediction becomes unreliable. The dotted line marks the actual type-I error from the oracle test that uses the true \\(\pi_0=0.5\\) directly (consistent with Figure 4B, it is exactly equal to its own prediction).

<center><p><img src="/figures/bin_CI_fig5a_unknowable.png" width="70%"></p></center>

Figure 5B generalises across all true \\(\pi\\) values from 0.1 to 0.9. Three quantities are plotted: the oracle prediction (green, from `binom_diff` with the true \\(\pi\\)), the mean \\(\hat\pi\\)-based prediction (red), and the actual rejection rate of the \\(\hat\pi\\)-based test (blue). The actual rejection rate is always conservative relative to \\(\alpha\\) — the \\(\hat\pi\\)-based test does not inflate type-I error — but the \\(\hat\pi\\)-based prediction is a poor proxy for how conservative it is. At some \\(\pi\\) values the prediction understates, and at others it overstates, the actual rejection rate.

<center><p><img src="/figures/bin_CI_fig5b_unknowable_by_pi.png" width="70%"></p></center>

This is the key contrast with the single-binomial case. In Section 1, the test statistic \\(y\\) and the null distribution \\(\text{Bin}(n, \pi_0)\\) share exactly the same parameter, so the CDF prediction is perfect. Here, \\(\hat\pi\\) is a noisy function of \\((y_1, y_2)\\), and the rejection bounds change sample-to-sample in a way that is entangled with the test statistic itself.

## (6) Power comparison

Having established the type-I properties, I turn to power. The simulation setting is \\(n_1=20\\), \\(n_2=25\\), with data generated as \\(y_1 \sim \text{Bin}(20, 0.5)\\) and \\(y_2 \sim \text{Bin}(25, \pi_2)\\) for \\(\pi_2\\) varying from 0.1 to 0.9. The null hypothesis is \\(H_0: \pi_1 = \pi_2\\), tested at \\(\alpha=5\%\\). When \\(\pi_2 = 0.5\\) we are under \\(H_0\\) and measuring type-I error; when \\(\pi_2 \neq 0.5\\) we are under the alternative and measuring power. Five methods are compared:

- **Exact-null**: `binom_diff` with the oracle \\(\pi_0 = 0.5\\)
- **Exact-hat**: `binom_diff` with the estimated \\(\hat\pi = (y_1+y_2)/(n_1+n_2)\\)
- **Fisher, Barnard**: `scipy.stats` exact tests
- **Newcomb**: `statsmodels` score CI rejection indicator

<center><p><img src="/figures/bin_CI_fig6_power_comparison.png" width="85%"></p></center>

Barnard's test is the most powerful exact method, which is consistent with theory: not conditioning on marginal totals preserves information. Fisher, which does condition, is slightly less powerful. The Newcomb method is competitive at this sample size, though recall from Section 3 that it can slightly exceed \\(\alpha\\) at extreme proportions. The Exact-null oracle has slightly lower power than Barnard — the `binom_diff` rejection region is less sharp than Barnard's optimised statistic — but it offers the knowably-conservative type-I interpretation from Section 4. The Exact-hat method is uniformly less powerful than Exact-null, because estimating \\(\pi_0\\) from the data adds noise to the rejection bounds and widens them on average.

For most applied settings, Barnard's exact test is the recommended choice: it is the most powerful exact test with strict type-I control and is available directly from `scipy.stats.barnard_exact`. The `binom_diff` approach is most useful when \\(\pi_0\\) is known from external context (e.g. a calibrated baseline rate) and a precisely characterised conservative type-I error is required.

## (7) Conclusion

This post developed an exact testing framework for the difference between two independent binomial proportions. The main findings are:

1. The `qbinom` conservative quantile-inversion approach gives knowably-conservative type-I errors for the single-binomial test, and these agree exactly with the Clopper-Pearson CI (Sections 1 and 1b).
2. The `binom_diff` class extends this to the two-sample setting when \\(\pi_0\\) is known, preserving the knowable-conservatism property (Section 4).
3. When \\(\pi_0\\) must be estimated using \\(\hat\pi\\), the knowable-conservatism guarantee breaks down: the test remains valid (type-I ≤ \\(\alpha\\)) but the degree of conservatism cannot be reliably predicted from the data (Section 5).
4. For most applied purposes, `scipy.stats.barnard_exact` provides the best combination of power and strict type-I control. The `binom_diff` exact-null approach is the right tool when interpretable conservatism bounds matter and \\(\pi_0\\) is available from prior information.
